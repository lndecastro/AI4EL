Module 4: Staying Smart with AI — Trust, Safety & Your Personal Toolkit#

Welcome to the final module of the AI for Everyday Life program.

Over the past three modules, you have built a substantial foundation. You understand what AI is and how it works. You can write effective prompts and use AI to draft emails, summarize documents, and analyze data. You have experimented with image generation, AI music, and presentation tools. You have a personal Prompt Library taking shape.

This final class is where we zoom out and ask the deeper questions that make the difference between someone who uses AI and someone who uses it wisely.

We will explore why AI goes wrong and how to catch it. We will build a clear, practical framework for privacy and responsible use. We will organize your personal AI toolkit. And we will close the program the way it began: with you at the center, curious, capable, and in control.

The central idea of this class: Being a great AI user is not about knowing the most tools. It is about maintaining your judgment, protecting your privacy, and building habits that serve you well as the technology keeps changing.

Learning Objectives#

By the end of this class, you will be able to:

  1. Explain why AI produces errors, including hallucinations, bias, and outdated information.

  2. Apply a practical verification framework to evaluate any AI output before acting on it.

  3. Identify what personal and professional information should never be shared with AI tools.

  4. Describe in plain language how AI tools use your data and what you can do about it.

  5. Apply core responsible AI principles to your own daily use.

  6. Articulate a realistic, balanced view of AI’s impact on work and employment.

  7. Assemble a personal AI toolkit matched to your actual needs and habits.

  8. Leave the program with a written Personal AI Action Plan you can start using immediately.

Part I — The Verification-First Mindset#

1.1 Why AI Gets Things Wrong#

AI tools are genuinely impressive but they are not infallible. Understanding why they make mistakes is the key to catching those mistakes before they cause problems.

Hallucinations

The most well-known AI failure mode is hallucination: the AI produces confident-sounding information that is simply false. This includes:

  • Inventing statistics, studies, or citations that do not exist.

  • Describing events that never happened.

  • Providing incorrect dates, names, or facts while sounding completely certain.

Why does this happen? AI language models do not “look up” facts. They generate the most statistically likely next word based on patterns in their training data. Sometimes those patterns lead to plausible-sounding but incorrect outputs. The model has no internal mechanism for knowing when it is wrong.

Warning

AI does not know when it is making things up. It sounds equally confident whether it is correct or completely wrong. You are the error-detection system.

Bias

AI systems learn from human-generated data, which means they absorb the biases, assumptions, and blind spots present in that data. This can appear as:

  • Stereotyped portrayals of people by gender, race, age, political preference, or profession.

  • Unequal quality of responses about different cultures or communities.

  • Assumptions baked into recommendations that reflect one group’s experience more than others’.

Understanding that AI reflects human biases, rather than being an objective authority, is essential for critical use.

Outdated Knowledge

Most AI models have a training cutoff date, that is, a point beyond which they have no knowledge of events. If you ask an AI about recent news, new laws, current prices, or anything that may have changed, you risk receiving confidently stated but outdated information.

Context Misunderstanding

AI takes your words literally. Ambiguous questions receive ambiguous answers. Missing context leads to missing relevance. The AI cannot read what you meant, only what you wrote.

1.2 The Trust or Verify Framework#

Not everything AI produces needs to be verified to the same degree. A good mental model is to match your level of scrutiny to the stakes involved.

Low stakes — spot-check:
Creative writing, brainstorming, informal emails, personal use. Read it, edit it if needed, and use it. A minor error in a birthday card invitation is not a crisis.

Medium stakes — review carefully:
Professional emails, summaries for colleagues, documents you will share, social media posts. Read every word, check key facts, and make sure the tone and content represent you accurately.

High stakes — verify independently:
Anything medical, legal, financial, or factual that others will rely on. Never use AI output for important decisions without independently checking key claims through authoritative sources.

Stakes Level

Examples

What to Do

1.3 A Practical Verification Toolkit#

When you need to verify an AI output, these strategies work:

Ask AI to explain itself:

You just told me [claim]. How confident are you in this? 
What sources support it? Is there anything you are uncertain about?

AI will often acknowledge uncertainty when directly asked, even if it did not volunteer it.

Ask AI to fact-check itself:

Please review your previous response and flag any claim that 
you are not fully certain about or that may have changed since 
your training data was collected.

Use a search engine or authoritative source:
For any specific fact, statistic, name, date, or event, Google it, check the original source, or consult a professional. A 30-second search can prevent a costly mistake.

Use a web-connected AI tool for current information:
Perplexity.ai, Copilot, or ChatGPT with web search enabled can retrieve current information and cite their sources, far more reliable for time-sensitive facts than a standard AI chat.

Apply the five-point quality check (from Class 2):

Criterion

Question

Relevance

Does the response actually address what I asked?

Completeness

Did it cover all the aspects I needed?

Clarity

Is it clear, readable, and well-structured?

Accuracy

Are the specific facts and figures correct? (Verify!)

Format

Did it follow the format I requested?

1.4 Activity 1: The Fact-Check Challenge#

Goal: Experience AI hallucination firsthand and practice catching it.

Step 1 — Ask AI a question with specific facts:
Choose one of these prompts and run it in any AI chat:

Option A: "Tell me about three scientific studies that prove [any health claim you have heard recently]."

Option B: "What are the current interest rates for a 30-year fixed mortgage in the United States?"

Option C: "Who is the current mayor of [name your city or a nearby city]?"

Option D: "Summarize a recent news article about [any topic in the news this month]."

Step 2 — Write down the specific claims AI made:
List at least three factual statements from its response: names, dates, numbers, sources, or events.

Step 3 — Verify each one:
For each claim, spend one to two minutes searching for independent confirmation. Use Google, a government website, a news outlet, or any authoritative source.

Step 4 — Record your findings:

| AI’s Claim | True / False / Partly True / Unverifiable | Source You Used | | | | | | | | | |

Reflect:

  • Were any claims wrong or unverifiable?

  • Did anything surprise you about the AI’s confidence level?

  • How would you have acted if you had used this information without checking?

💡 Key insight: This exercise is not meant to make you distrust AI — it is meant to make you a calibrated user who knows when to trust and when to verify. Both skills matter equally.

Part II — Privacy, Safety & Responsible Use#

2.1 What Not to Share with AI Tools#

Every time you type something into an AI chat, that text becomes input to the system — and depending on the platform’s policies, may be stored, used to improve the model, reviewed by humans, or retained for extended periods.

This does not mean AI tools are dangerous. It means they require the same thoughtfulness about sharing information as any online platform.

What to keep private — always:

Category

Examples

Personal identification

Social Security numbers, passport numbers, driver’s license numbers

Financial information

Bank account details, credit card numbers, PINs, financial passwords

Login credentials

Passwords, security questions and answers

Sensitive personal health

Detailed medical records or conditions (especially if combined with identifying information)

Private information about others

Other people’s personal details without their knowledge or consent

Confidential professional information

Client names, proprietary business data, internal company documents, trade secrets

Children’s information

Never share identifying information about minors

A practical test before you type:
“Would I be comfortable if this text were posted publicly, or reviewed by an employee of this company?”
If the answer is no — do not type it.

2.2 How Your Data is Used: Plain Language#

Different AI platforms have different data policies. Here are the most important things to know:

Free tiers often use your data for training:
On many free AI platforms, conversations may be used to improve the model. This means human reviewers or automated systems may access your inputs. Check the privacy settings — most platforms allow you to opt out of training data collection.

Paid tiers and enterprise plans typically offer stronger privacy:
Business and enterprise versions of most major AI tools (ChatGPT Enterprise, Claude for Work, Copilot for Microsoft 365) typically offer stronger data protection, including commitments not to use your data for training.

Incognito or temporary modes help:
ChatGPT and some other platforms offer a mode where conversations are not saved. Use this for any sensitive discussion.

Where to find your privacy settings:

Platform

Where to Find It

Claude

💡 Simple rule: Treat your AI chat window like a postcard, not a sealed envelope. Write accordingly.

2.3 AI at Work: Organizational Responsibilities#

If you use AI tools in a professional context, there is an additional layer of responsibility:

Check your organization’s AI policy before you start.
Many companies, schools, hospitals, and government agencies have policies about which AI tools are approved for use, what kinds of data can be processed by them, and what disclosures are required when AI-generated content is used.

Never input client, patient, or student data into a public AI tool
without explicit organizational approval. This applies to names, case details, medical records, academic records, and any other information that belongs to the people your organization serves.

Be transparent with colleagues and clients
when AI has played a significant role in producing work product. This is good professional ethics — and increasingly, it is good risk management.

Maintain your professional judgment.
AI does not hold a license. Doctors, lawyers, accountants, engineers, educators, and other professionals are still responsible for the advice and work they produce — even if AI assisted in producing it.

2.4 Core Principles for Responsible Everyday AI Use#

Drawn from leading AI ethics frameworks and adapted for everyday users, these five principles can guide every AI interaction:

1. Fairness — Be aware that AI may reflect biases, and actively consider whether outputs treat all people equitably. Do not use AI in ways that discriminate or exclude.

2. Transparency — Be honest when AI played a significant role in producing content you share. Disclose AI assistance in contexts where it is relevant — professionally, academically, or publicly.

3. Accountability — You are responsible for what you do with AI outputs. The AI is the tool; you are the decision-maker. Own the outcomes.

4. Safety — Verify before you act. Never rely on unverified AI output for high-stakes decisions involving health, finances, safety, or legal matters.

5. Human dignity — Never use AI to demean, deceive, manipulate, or harm other people. This includes generating fake content about real people, spreading AI-generated misinformation, or using AI to impersonate others.

2.5 AI and Jobs: A Realistic, Reassuring View#

One of the most common concerns people bring to this program is: “Will AI take my job?”

The most accurate and honest answer, based on current evidence and expert consensus, is: it depends on the job, and the change will be gradual.

Here is what the evidence actually suggests:

AI is more likely to change jobs than eliminate them.
Most roles involve a mix of tasks — some of which AI can assist with, others where human judgment, relationships, and physical presence remain essential. AI tends to automate specific tasks, not entire jobs.

The jobs most affected are those involving repetitive, pattern-based cognitive work.
Highly routine document processing, basic data entry, simple customer queries — these are already being changed. The humans in those roles who use AI effectively will be better positioned than those who do not.

New roles are also being created.
AI trainers, prompt engineers, AI ethics reviewers, AI output editors, and AI literacy educators are all emerging roles that did not exist five years ago.

Adaptability is the most durable career skill.
Learning to work effectively with AI — as you are doing in this program — is precisely the kind of adaptability that will matter most over the next decade.

The future of work is not AI instead of people. It is AI with people — where the people who understand how to use AI thoughtfully and ethically will have a real advantage.

3.2 Free vs. Paid: What Is Worth It?#

Most people starting out can accomplish a great deal with free tiers. Here is an honest assessment:

Free tiers are excellent for:

  • Drafting and editing text

  • Answering questions and brainstorming

  • Basic image generation

  • Summarizing documents

  • Learning and experimenting

Paid tiers are worth considering when:

  • You hit daily or message limits on the free tier regularly

  • You need to process longer documents (paid tiers typically allow much longer inputs)

  • You need stronger privacy guarantees (especially for professional use)

  • You need access to the most capable model versions

  • You are using AI heavily enough that the time savings justify the cost (typically $20–30/month)

Our recommendation: Start free. Use the tools for 4–6 weeks. If you are regularly hitting limits or want more capability, then consider a paid plan.

3.3 Activity 2: Set Up Your Personal AI Toolkit#

Goal: Leave this class with a working, personalized AI setup.

Step 1 — Choose your primary AI tool
Based on the tools you have tried in this program, pick one general AI chatbot as your main tool. Create an account if you have not already.

Step 2 — Adjust your privacy settings
Using the table in Section 2.2, find and review the data/privacy settings for your chosen tool. Make any adjustments you are comfortable with (e.g., opting out of training data use).

Step 3 — Organize your Prompt Library
Create a simple document, note, or folder where you save your tested prompts from across all four classes. Organize them by category:

  • Emails and communication

  • Document summarizing and creation

  • Spreadsheet and data tasks

  • Creative tasks (images, presentations, music)

  • Research and fact-checking

Step 4 — Bookmark your tools
Create a browser bookmark folder called “My AI Toolkit” with links to the two or three tools you plan to use regularly.

Step 5 — Set a reminder
Schedule one 20-minute “AI experiment” session per week for the next month. Use it to try a new use case, test a new prompt, or simply practice with a task from your week.

Part IV — Building Habits That Last#

4.1 The AI-Aware → AI-Enabled Progression#

AI literacy is not a destination — it is a direction. There are three recognizable stages in how people develop their relationship with AI:

Stage 1 — AI-Aware
You know AI exists and is increasingly part of life. You may feel curious, cautious, or uncertain. You are not yet using it regularly. This was where most participants started this program.

Stage 2 — AI-Engaged
You are experimenting regularly. You have a few tools you turn to for specific tasks. You sometimes get great results, sometimes not — and you are learning from both. This is where you are now.

Stage 3 — AI-Enabled
AI is a natural, integrated part of how you work and create. You know which tools to use, when to trust the output, and when to verify or discard it. You are continuously learning as the tools evolve. This is where consistent practice takes you.

The distance between Stage 2 and Stage 3 is not talent — it is regular, intentional practice.

4.2 Three Habits of Effective AI Users#

After four classes of practice, three habits stand out as the most impactful for long-term AI effectiveness:

Habit 1: Reach for AI early in the process, not at the end.
Use AI to help you start — to generate a first draft, brainstorm options, or frame a problem. This is where it saves the most time. Using it to polish something already written is valuable but less transformative than using it to overcome the blank-page problem.

Habit 2: Always read what AI gives you before using it.
Treat every AI output as a draft, not a final product. This takes 30 seconds for short content and a few minutes for longer pieces — and it is the single most important habit for avoiding embarrassing errors.

Habit 3: Keep a running prompt library and add to it weekly.
Every time you craft a prompt that works really well, save it. Over weeks and months, your personal Prompt Library becomes one of the most valuable professional tools you own — because it is tailored to exactly your work, your voice, and your needs.

Part V — Program Wrap-Up and Reflection#

5.1 Looking Back Across Four Modules#

Let us take a moment to acknowledge how far you have come.

Module 1 — Understanding AI Without the Fear
You learned what AI actually is, how it learns from patterns, what types of AI exist, and where it already appears in your daily life. You wrote your first prompts and had your first AI conversations.

Module 2 — AI as Your Daily Assistant
You learned prompt patterns and context engineering. You drafted and improved emails, summarized documents, and used AI to make sense of spreadsheet data. You built the foundation of a personal Prompt Library.

Module 3 — AI Gets Creative
You generated images, built AI-assisted presentations, experimented with AI music, and explored avatar and voice tools. You developed a practical framework for copyright, transparency, and responsible creative use.

Module 4 — Staying Smart with AI
You deepened your understanding of why AI fails and how to catch those failures. You built a clear framework for privacy and responsible use. You assembled your personal AI toolkit and wrote a concrete action plan.

That is a complete arc — from curiosity to capability, from confusion to confidence.

5.2 What You Have Built#

By completing this program, you now have:

  • AI literacy — a solid, honest understanding of what AI is and what it is not.

  • Prompt fluency — the ability to communicate effectively with AI tools across a wide range of tasks.

  • A personal Prompt Library — tested, reusable prompts tailored to your real needs.

  • Creative AI skills — the ability to generate images, presentations, music, and more.

  • A verification habit — the practice of reviewing and checking AI outputs before acting on them.

  • A privacy framework — clear rules for what to share and what to protect.

  • A personal AI toolkit — the right two or three tools set up and ready to use.

  • A Personal AI Action Plan — a written commitment to the habits that will sustain your growth.

These are not abstract skills. They are practical, transferable, and immediately useful — today, in the life you are actually living.

5.3 Final Reflection#

Take a few minutes to write your honest answers to these questions. You were asked some of them at the start of Class 1. See how your thinking may have shifted.

Looking back at where you started, what has changed most in how you think about AI?

What is the one thing from this program you are most likely to use every single week?

What questions do you still have — about AI, about specific tools, or about how this technology will evolve?

What would you tell a friend or family member who says: “I don’t think AI is for people like me”?

What responsibility do you feel as someone who now understands AI better than most of the people around you?

There is no right answer to any of these. The fact that you are asking them — thoughtfully, critically, from a place of genuine understanding — is the whole point.

5.4 Continuing Your Learning#

AI is evolving fast. The tools available today will look different in six months, and significantly different in two years. The best way to stay current is not to take another course every few months — it is to stay curious and keep experimenting.

Ways to continue growing:

  • Read widely — Follow one or two trusted voices on AI in plain language. Ethan Mollick’s newsletter One Useful Thing (oneusefulthing.org) is one of the best.

  • Experiment weekly — Your 20-minute weekly AI session is the single most effective way to keep growing.

  • Explore the FGCU AI Academy — The AI Literacy Program and Data Analysis with AI Program offer deeper dives into the concepts introduced here. The Summer Academy offers intensive, structured pathways for learners who want to go further.

  • Share what you learn — Teaching is the fastest way to deepen your own understanding. The person you named in your Action Plan is a great place to start.

  • Stay connected to the Dendritic Institute — Visit fgcu.edu/eng/dendritic-institute for upcoming workshops, events, and resources.
